Walk into any supermarket in Lagos and you'll find a long aisle of juice cartons, many with bright pictures of oranges and pineapples on the front. Then there's fresh juice, pressed the same day and sold cold. Both are called "juice", but they're very different products. So what's the real difference in fresh juice vs packaged juice, and how do you know what you're actually drinking?

This guide explains it plainly. We're not here to knock anyone's favourite carton. Packaged juice has its place, especially when you need something that keeps. We just think you deserve to know what's in the glass.

## Fresh juice vs packaged juice: the short version

- **Fresh juice** is pressed from fruit and vegetables, kept cold, and drunk within days.
- **Packaged juice** is processed so it can sit on a shelf or in a warehouse for weeks or months.

Most of the differences come from that one fact: how long the juice needs to last.

## What "from concentrate" means

Many packaged juices are made from concentrate. Here's how that usually works:

1. Fruit is juiced, often far from where the final drink is sold.
2. Most of the water is removed, usually by heating and evaporation, leaving a thick concentrate.
3. The concentrate is stored and shipped, which is cheaper than moving full-volume juice.
4. Later, water is added back to "reconstitute" it into juice.

Along the way, some of the natural aroma and flavour can be lost. That's why some producers add flavourings back in. A juice from concentrate can still be 100% juice, but it has travelled a long way from the fruit.

Fresh juice skips all of this. The fruit goes in, the juice comes out, and it goes into a bottle.

## Pasteurisation: why most packaged juice is heated

Pasteurisation means heating juice to kill bacteria and help it last longer. It's a proven food-safety process and an important one for any juice that has to sit on a shelf.

The trade-off is that heat can affect some of the more delicate parts of fruit, such as vitamin C and the fresh, bright flavour you get from just-cut fruit. Studies suggest that heat treatment can reduce levels of some heat-sensitive vitamins, though how much depends on the method and the fruit.

Fresh, cold-pressed juice isn't heated. Instead, it relies on cold: it's kept refrigerated from the moment it's pressed until you drink it. That's why fresh juice has a short life and must stay in the fridge. You can read more about how cold-pressing works in [why cold-pressed matters](/blog/why-cold-pressed).

## Added sugar: juice, nectar and "juice drink"

This is where labels matter most. Not everything in a juice carton is the same product:

- **100% juice** should contain only juice, either fresh or reconstituted from concentrate.
- **Nectar** is usually a mix of fruit juice or purée, water and sugar or sweetener.
- **Juice drink** or **fruit drink** can contain a fairly small amount of juice, with water, sugar, flavourings and colours making up the rest.

The picture on the front may look the same for all three. The ingredients list on the back tells the real story.

Even 100% juice contains natural fruit sugar, so moderation still matters. But a drink with added sugar on top of fruit sugar adds up quickly. If you're watching your sugar, our [honest guide to juice and weight loss](/blog/best-juice-for-weight-loss-nigeria) goes into more detail.

## Shelf life: the clue in the date

Shelf life tells you a lot about how a juice has been made.

- **Long shelf life (weeks or months, unopened, out of the fridge):** the juice has almost certainly been heat-treated and sealed to keep it stable. Some products also contain preservatives.
- **Short shelf life (a few days, refrigerated):** this points to fresh juice that has not been heat-treated or preserved.

Our juices, for example, should be kept refrigerated at 2–6°C and drunk within 4 days. That short window is the price of skipping heat and preservatives, and we think it's worth it.

## How to read a juice label

Next time you pick up a carton or bottle, turn it around and check these things:

### 1. The product name

Look for the exact wording: "100% juice", "nectar" or "juice drink". These words matter more than the picture.

### 2. The ingredients list

Ingredients are listed from most to least. If water or sugar comes before any fruit, the drink is mostly water or sugar.

### 3. Words that mean sugar

Sugar hides under many names: sucrose, glucose, glucose syrup, fructose syrup, dextrose, invert sugar and cane sugar. Any of these means sugar has been added.

### 4. "From concentrate"

This isn't a bad thing on its own, but it tells you the juice has been concentrated and reconstituted.

### 5. Preservatives and additives

Look for preservatives such as sodium benzoate or potassium sorbate, plus added colours and flavourings.

### 6. Storage instructions

"Store in a cool, dry place" means shelf-stable. "Keep refrigerated" and a short use-by date usually mean fresh.

## Which is right for you?

There's no single right answer. Packaged 100% juice is convenient, travels well and keeps for long periods. It can be useful for a long trip or for keeping a stock at home.

Fresh juice is for when you want juice as close to the fruit as possible: no heat, no added sugar, no preservatives and the full, bright flavour of fresh produce. It needs a fridge and needs drinking quickly.
