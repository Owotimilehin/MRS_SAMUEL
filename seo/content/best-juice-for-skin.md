No juice will clear acne or erase wrinkles. But your skin needs vitamin C to make collagen, and studies link eating more fruit and vegetables with healthier-looking skin colour. Juices made from orange, carrot, pawpaw and guava supply vitamin C and carotenoids. Sunscreen, sleep and a balanced diet still matter most.

"Which juice is good for glowing skin?" is one of the questions we hear most, usually from brides-to-be and anyone with a big event coming. Social media is full of carrot-and-beetroot recipes that promise flawless skin in two weeks. Here is what the research actually says, which nutrients matter, which juices carry them, and where the claims go too far.

## How food affects your skin

Your skin is your largest organ, and like the rest of your body it is built from what you eat. Two groups of nutrients have the most research behind them for skin.

**Vitamin C**

Normal skin contains high levels of vitamin C. A [2017 review in the journal Nutrients](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28805671/) explains that vitamin C helps your body make collagen, the protein that gives skin its strength and firmness, and helps protect skin from damage caused by sunlight. The [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminC-Consumer/) also notes that your body needs vitamin C to make collagen and to heal wounds. Your body cannot make vitamin C, so it must come from food every day.

The same review points out that we still do not know exactly how much extra vitamin C helps skin in people who already get enough. Topping up beyond normal needs is not proven to give better skin.

**Carotenoids**

Carotenoids are the orange, red and yellow pigments in carrots, pawpaw, tomatoes and other produce. Some, like beta-carotene, your body can turn into vitamin A, which skin needs for normal growth and repair. The [Linus Pauling Institute at Oregon State University](https://lpi.oregonstate.edu/mic/health-disease/skin-health) has a detailed summary of how vitamins and plant compounds relate to skin.

Carotenoids also collect in the skin itself. A [2015 study in Nutrients](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26184306/) found that young women who ate more fruit and vegetables had a slightly more golden skin tone, and earlier research suggested people rate this tone as healthier looking. That study was small and only included women of one skin type, so we cannot say the same effect shows on darker Nigerian skin. Treat it as interesting, not proven.

## What juice can do for your skin

Put together, here is the fair summary:

- **It can help you meet your vitamin C needs.** A glass of fresh orange or guava-based juice is an easy source.
- **It can add carotenoids**, especially from carrot and pawpaw.
- **It adds fluid.** Hydration matters for your whole body. However, there is little good evidence that drinking more fluid than you need makes skin look better.
- **It can replace sugary drinks.** A diet high in added sugar is not good for your overall health, and swapping soft drinks for something with real nutrients is a step in the right direction.

## What juice cannot do for your skin

- **It cannot clear acne.** Acne has many causes, including hormones and genes. If acne is affecting you, see a doctor or dermatologist. There are effective treatments.
- **It cannot replace sunscreen.** Vitamin C supports the skin's own defences, but it is not a sunscreen. Sun damage is a major cause of skin ageing, as the [Linus Pauling Institute](https://lpi.oregonstate.edu/mic/health-disease/skin-health) explains.
- **It cannot "detox" your skin.** Your liver and kidneys clear waste. The [NIH's NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/detoxes-and-cleanses-what-you-need-to-know) says there is no compelling evidence for "detox" diets.
- **It cannot lighten or bleach skin.** Be very wary of any drink or product that promises to. Any change from carotenoids is subtle and goes the other way, towards a warmer tone.
- **It does not beat whole fruit.** Whole fruit gives you the same vitamins plus fibre. [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/nutrition-and-healthy-eating/expert-answers/juicing/faq-20058020) says there is little evidence juice is healthier than eating the produce itself.

## The best juice ingredients for skin

If you want a juice that supports your skin, look for these on the label:

- **Orange**: a classic source of vitamin C.
- **Guava**: very high in vitamin C.
- **Pineapple**: a good source of vitamin C.
- **Carrot**: rich in beta-carotene.
- **Pawpaw**: carries both vitamin C and carotenoids.
- **Watermelon and tomato**: contain lycopene, the red carotenoid.
- **Beetroot**: adds colour and plant compounds, though its skin benefits are not well studied.

The best choice is often a mix: one fruit for vitamin C, one orange or red vegetable for carotenoids.

## Habits that matter more than any juice

If clear, healthy skin is your goal, these do more than any single drink:

1. **Wear sunscreen daily**, even on cloudy Lagos days, and reapply when you are out in the sun.
2. **Sleep enough.** Skin repairs itself overnight.
3. **Eat a varied diet** with plenty of whole fruit, vegetables, beans and fish.
4. **Drink water** through the day.
5. **Do not smoke.** Smoking harms your overall health, skin included.
6. **See a doctor** for acne, eczema, dark patches or any skin change that worries you.

Juice fits in as one small part of point three.

## How much to drink

A small glass a day is enough. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/5-a-day/5-a-day-what-counts/) suggests limiting fruit juice, vegetable juice and smoothies to a combined total of 150ml a day, drunk with a meal, because juicing releases the sugars in fruit and these can damage teeth. The [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news/item/04-03-2015-who-calls-on-countries-to-reduce-sugars-intake-among-adults-and-children) counts the sugars in juice as "free sugars" to keep low.

If you have diabetes, take regular medicine, or are pregnant, talk to your doctor before adding a daily juice.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which juice is best for glowing skin?

A juice that combines vitamin C fruits, like orange, guava or pineapple, with carotenoid-rich vegetables, like carrot or pawpaw, gives your skin useful nutrients. No juice guarantees glowing skin. Sunscreen, sleep and a varied diet matter more.

### Can juice clear acne?

No. Acne has many causes, including hormones and genes, and no juice has been shown to clear it. If acne bothers you, see a doctor or dermatologist, as there are effective treatments.

### Is carrot juice good for the skin?

Carrot juice is rich in beta-carotene, which your body can turn into vitamin A, and carotenoids collect in the skin. Small studies link more fruit and vegetables with a healthier-looking skin tone. It is a good ingredient, not a cure.

### Does vitamin C juice help with collagen?

Your body needs vitamin C to make collagen, so getting enough matters for skin. But there is no strong evidence that extra vitamin C beyond your normal needs gives you better skin.

### How long before juice shows on my skin?

There is no reliable timeline. Studies on fruit, vegetables and skin colour looked at diets over weeks to months, not days. Think of juice as part of a long-term healthy diet rather than a quick fix before an event.
