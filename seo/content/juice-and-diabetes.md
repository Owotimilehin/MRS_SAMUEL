If you have diabetes, whole fruit is a better choice than juice, and water is the best everyday drink. Juice, even with no added sugar, raises blood sugar quickly because the fibre is gone. If you drink it, keep it to a small glass with a meal, choose vegetable-led blends, and agree the plan with your doctor or dietitian.

We make juice for a living, and we are going to tell you something that may sound strange from a juice company: **if you have diabetes, you probably should not drink much juice.** Diabetes is common in Nigeria, and many of our customers or their parents live with it. You deserve honest information, not a sales pitch. This guide explains why juice and diabetes need care, what research says, and how to make safer choices if you do want the occasional glass.

**Please read this first:** this guide is general information, not medical advice. Diabetes care is personal. Your doctor, diabetes nurse or a registered dietitian knows your medicines, your blood sugar pattern and your other health needs. Speak to them before changing what you eat or drink.

## Why juice is different from whole fruit

When you eat an orange, you chew through its pulp and fibre. When you drink orange juice, most of that insoluble fibre has been left behind in the juicer.

Fibre matters for blood sugar. It slows how quickly sugar moves from your gut into your blood, and it helps you feel full. The [Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/carbohydrates/carbohydrates-and-blood-sugar/) explains how foods that are digested quickly cause faster, larger rises in blood sugar. Without fibre, juice is digested fast.

Juice also makes it easy to take in a lot of fruit at once. A glass can hold the juice of several oranges, which you would struggle to eat in one sitting.

The [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news/item/04-03-2015-who-calls-on-countries-to-reduce-sugars-intake-among-adults-and-children) counts the sugars naturally present in fruit juice as "free sugars", in the same group as added table sugar, and advises keeping them low. "No added sugar" on a bottle does not mean "no sugar".

## What the research says

Large studies point the same way:

- **Whole fruit vs juice.** A [2013 study in the BMJ](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23990623/) followed more than 187,000 American health professionals for many years. Eating more of certain whole fruits, especially blueberries, grapes and apples, was linked with a lower risk of developing type 2 diabetes. Drinking more fruit juice was linked with a higher risk.
- **Juice and diabetes risk.** A [2015 review in the BMJ](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26199070/) pooled studies on sugary drinks, diet drinks and fruit juice. It found that higher intake of sugar-sweetened drinks was linked with more type 2 diabetes. Fruit juice also showed a link, though the authors said that finding was likely affected by bias. They still concluded that fruit juice was unlikely to be a healthy alternative to sugary drinks.

These studies looked at the risk of developing diabetes, not at people who already have it. They show links, not proof of cause. But they help explain why diabetes guidance treats juice with caution.

The [NIH's National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK)](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/diabetes/overview/healthy-living-with-diabetes) lists juice alongside soda, flavoured coffee and sports drinks among the sweet drinks to have less of when planning meals with diabetes. The [Harvard Nutrition Source](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/healthy-drinks/) advises everyone to avoid sugary drinks and to limit juice to one small glass a day, and that advice is general, not specific to diabetes.

## Our honest advice

If you have diabetes or prediabetes:

1. **Make water your main drink.** Plain water, or water with a slice of lemon or cucumber, does not raise your blood sugar.
2. **Choose whole fruit over juice.** Agree with your doctor or dietitian which fruits and how much suit you. Whole fruit brings fibre that juice loses.
3. **If you drink juice, treat it as an occasional small serving, not a daily habit.** Many people with diabetes will be advised to limit it a lot, or avoid it.
4. **Never use juice as a "healthy" way to quench thirst.** That glass after a hot walk through Balogun market adds up quickly.
5. **Check your blood sugar** if your doctor has asked you to, to see how a food or drink affects you personally.

## If you and your doctor agree to the occasional glass

Some people with well-controlled diabetes may be told a small amount of juice now and then is fine. If that is you, these steps may reduce the blood sugar spike:

- **Keep it small.** The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/5-a-day/5-a-day-what-counts/) suggests a combined maximum of 150ml of juice or smoothie a day for the general public. Your own limit may be lower. Ask your dietitian.
- **Drink it with a meal**, not on its own. Food with protein, fat and fibre in the same meal can slow how quickly sugar is absorbed.
- **Choose vegetable-led juices.** Juices made mostly from cucumber, celery, leafy greens, tomato or pepper carry fewer sweet fruits than pineapple, orange or mango juices.
- **Avoid juices with added sugar**, syrups, concentrates or "juice drinks". Read the label.
- **Count it.** Juice contains carbohydrate. Include it in your carbohydrate count or meal plan, as your diabetes team has taught you.
- **Sip it, don't gulp it**, and do not drink a whole large bottle in one go.

## A special case: low blood sugar (hypos)

There is one situation where juice can be useful for people with diabetes: treating a hypo, when blood sugar drops too low. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/low-blood-sugar-hypoglycaemia/) advises that if you have diabetes and your blood sugar is below 4mmol/L, or you have hypo symptoms, you should eat or drink something that raises it quickly, such as a small glass of fruit juice, and then check your sugar again after 10 to 15 minutes.

Follow the hypo plan your own doctor or diabetes nurse gives you. Fresh juice has no added sugar and its sugar content varies from batch to batch, so ask your team whether a standard glucose source would be more reliable for you.

## Watch out for "diabetic juice" claims

You may see drinks sold as "good for diabetes", "sugar-balancing" or even claims that a herbal juice can replace medicine. Be very careful.

- No juice treats or cures diabetes.
- Never stop or reduce your diabetes medicine because of a drink, a herb or a cleanse.
- The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/type-2-diabetes/food-and-keeping-active/) warns people with type 2 diabetes not to switch to a very strict, low-calorie diet without talking to a health professional first, as this may not be suitable if you take insulin or other diabetes medicines. That includes juice cleanses.
- Some ingredients, like bitter leaf, ginger or hibiscus, are often promoted for blood sugar or blood pressure, but the evidence is limited and some may interact with medicines. Ask your doctor before using them regularly.

The [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/diabetes) notes that diabetes can be treated with diet, physical activity and medication, and its consequences avoided through regular screening for complications and timely treatment. That is the plan that works.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can a diabetic drink fresh juice?

Some people with diabetes may be able to have a small glass occasionally, with a meal, as part of a plan agreed with their doctor or dietitian. Many will be advised to limit it a lot. Whole fruit and water are better everyday choices. Always ask your diabetes team first.

### Is fresh juice with no added sugar safe for diabetes?

"No added sugar" still means natural sugar. The WHO counts sugars in fruit juice as free sugars, and without fibre they raise blood sugar quickly. Fresh juice is better than sweetened drinks, but it is not sugar-free.

### Which juice is best for diabetics?

If your doctor or dietitian agrees to juice at all, a small glass of a vegetable-led juice, such as cucumber, leafy greens, tomato or pepper with little fruit, is likely to carry less sugar than a fruit-only juice. Water and whole fruit remain better choices.

### Is whole fruit better than juice for diabetes?

Yes. Whole fruit keeps its fibre, which slows sugar absorption and helps you feel full. A large BMJ study linked certain whole fruits with lower type 2 diabetes risk and fruit juice with higher risk.

### Can juice help with low blood sugar?

Yes, in that specific situation. The NHS lists a small glass of fruit juice as one way to raise blood sugar quickly during a hypo. Follow your own diabetes team's hypo plan and check your sugar again after 10 to 15 minutes.

### Can juice cure or reverse diabetes?

No. No juice treats, cures or reverses diabetes. Never stop or change your medicine because of a drink. Diabetes is managed with diet, activity, medicine and regular check-ups with your doctor.
