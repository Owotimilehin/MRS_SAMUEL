A juice cleanse means drinking only juice for one to several days. Research does not support the claim that it removes toxins: your liver and kidneys already do that. Any quick weight loss is mostly water and comes back. Juice works better alongside normal meals than as a replacement for them.

We sell juice, so you might expect us to tell you a three-day cleanse will change your life. We won't. Every few weeks someone asks us for a "detox" before a wedding, after the Christmas rice, or because a friend lost five kilos on one. You deserve a straight answer. Here is what a juice cleanse is, what the research says, who should never try one, and how to use fresh juice sensibly instead.

## What is a juice cleanse?

A juice cleanse (also called a juice fast or juice detox) is a short plan where you drink only fruit and vegetable juices, usually five or six bottles a day, for one to seven days. Some plans add water, herbal tea or nut milk. Most cut out solid food completely.

The claims around cleanses usually include:

- "Flushing out toxins"
- "Resetting" your gut or metabolism
- Fast weight loss
- Clearer skin and more energy

Let's look at each one against the evidence.

## Does a juice cleanse remove toxins?

No good evidence says it does. Your liver, kidneys, gut, lungs and skin clear waste from your body all day, every day, whether you drink juice or not.

The [NIH National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH)](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/detoxes-and-cleanses-what-you-need-to-know) reviewed the research on detox programmes. It found that the few studies done were mostly of low quality, and that a 2015 review found no compelling research to support "detox" diets for weight management or for eliminating toxins from the body. Most "detox" products never even say which toxins they remove.

[Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/nutrition-and-healthy-eating/expert-answers/juicing/faq-20058020) also says there is no sound scientific evidence that juice cleanses remove toxins, and notes that they may not give your body enough nutrition.

## Does a juice cleanse help you lose weight?

You may see the scale drop, but it rarely lasts. NCCIH notes that a 2017 review found juicing and "detox" diets can cause initial weight loss because you eat fewer calories, but that people tend to regain the weight once they return to a normal diet.

Much of the early drop is water and the food sitting in your gut, not fat. Juice also has little protein and little fibre, so many people feel hungry, tired and irritable by day two. That often ends in a big plate of jollof, which is fair enough.

If you want lasting weight change, the honest route is a steady diet you can keep up, with more vegetables, less sugar and regular movement. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/type-2-diabetes/food-and-keeping-active/) specifically warns people with type 2 diabetes not to switch to a very strict, low-calorie diet without talking to a health professional first.

## Does a juice cleanse "reset" your gut?

There is little evidence for this, and some evidence against. Juicing removes most of the insoluble fibre that feeds your gut bacteria. A small [2025 study in the journal Nutrients](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39940316/) gave 14 adults a juice-only diet, juice plus food, or a plant-based food diet for three days. The juice-only diet was linked with changes in mouth bacteria, and the researchers suggested short-term juice-only diets may negatively affect the microbiome, likely because of high sugar and low fibre. It was a small study, but it does not support the "gut reset" idea.

## Can a juice cleanse be harmful?

For a healthy adult, a day of drinking mostly juice is unlikely to cause lasting harm, though you may feel hungry and tired. But cleanses carry real risks for some people. NCCIH lists several:

- **Unpasteurised juice and infection.** Fresh juice that has not been pasteurised or treated can make people sick. The risk is highest for children, older people, pregnant women and anyone with a weak immune system.
- **Oxalate and kidney stones.** Some cleanse juices are high in oxalate, from leafy greens and beets. People prone to kidney stones should limit high-oxalate foods. Drinking large amounts of these juices for days is not wise.
- **Laxatives and colon cleansing.** Some "detox" plans add laxatives or enemas, which can cause dehydration and other serious side effects. Avoid any plan that includes these.

Juice-only plans also carry a lot of sugar with little to slow it down, which matters if you have diabetes.

## Who should not do a juice cleanse

Please do not start a juice-only plan without talking to your doctor if you:

- Have diabetes or prediabetes, or take medicine that lowers blood sugar
- Have kidney disease or have had kidney stones
- Are pregnant or breastfeeding
- Take warfarin or other regular medicine (leafy greens are high in vitamin K, which can affect warfarin, as the [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminK-Consumer/) explains)
- Have a history of eating disorders
- Are under 18 or elderly
- Have a stomach, bowel or heart condition

If you are unsure, ask your doctor or a registered dietitian. That is not a formality. It matters.

## A gentler way to use fresh juice

We would rather you drank one good juice a day for a year than six a day for three days. Here is an approach that fits the evidence:

1. **Keep eating meals.** Have your juice with breakfast or lunch, not instead of it.
2. **Keep the portion small.** The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/5-a-day/5-a-day-what-counts/) suggests a combined total of 150ml of fruit juice, vegetable juice or smoothie a day, because juicing releases the sugars in fruit.
3. **Choose vegetable-led juices** if sugar is a concern. Cucumber, celery and leafy greens carry less sugar than sweet fruits.
4. **Swap, don't add.** The biggest win is replacing a daily soft drink or sweetened "juice drink" with something real.
5. **Drink water.** Water is still the best everyday drink in Lagos heat.
6. **Eat whole fruit.** Pawpaw, oranges, guava and watermelon from the market bring fibre that juice cannot.

The [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news/item/04-03-2015-who-calls-on-countries-to-reduce-sugars-intake-among-adults-and-children) counts the sugars in fruit juice as "free sugars", which adults and children should keep low. That is the main reason we say a glass, not a crate.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a juice cleanse really detox your body?

No good evidence shows that it does. Your liver and kidneys remove waste every day. The NIH's NCCIH says research does not support "detox" diets for removing toxins, and most products never say which toxins they target.

### How much weight can you lose on a juice cleanse?

You may lose some weight in a few days, mostly water and gut contents, because you eat fewer calories. Research reviewed by NCCIH shows people tend to regain it after going back to normal food. Lasting change comes from a diet you can keep up.

### Is a 3-day juice cleanse safe?

For many healthy adults it is unlikely to cause lasting harm, but you may feel hungry, tired and light-headed. It is not safe for everyone. If you have diabetes, kidney problems, are pregnant or take regular medicine, talk to your doctor first.

### Can I do a juice cleanse if I have diabetes?

Please do not try a juice-only plan without your doctor's advice. Juice raises blood sugar quickly and a cleanse can interfere with diabetes medicines. Read our [guide to juice and diabetes](/blog/juice-and-diabetes) and speak to your doctor or dietitian.

### What should I do instead of a juice cleanse?

Keep eating balanced meals, add one small glass of fresh, vegetable-led juice with a meal, drink water, eat whole fruit and vegetables, and cut back on soft drinks. It is less dramatic than a cleanse, but it is what the evidence supports.
