Kunu is a family of northern Nigerian drinks. Kunu zaki, the most common, is made from millet or sorghum, spiced with ginger and cloves, lightly fermented and strained into a thin, sweet-sour drink. Other types use groundnut and rice (kunu gyada) or tigernut (kunu aya). All are filling, but freshness and hygiene matter.

If you grew up in Kaduna, Kano or Jos, kunu needs no introduction. In Lagos, most people know it from a cold sachet or bottle bought in traffic. But "kunu" is not one drink. Here is a clear guide to the main types, what goes into them, how to make kunu zaki at home and how to enjoy it without the stomach wahala.

## What does "kunu" mean?

In Hausa, **kunu** (or kunun) means a thin drink or gruel, usually made from grain. The word after it tells you the type: **zaki** means sweet, **gyada** means groundnut, **aya** means tigernut. So "kunun zaki" is simply "sweet kunu".

Recipes change from town to town and from family to family, so treat the descriptions below as the common versions, not fixed rules.

## The main types of kunu

**Kunu zaki (sweet kunu)**

The one most Lagosians mean when they say kunu. It is made from millet, sorghum (guinea corn) or a mix, with ginger, cloves and sometimes black pepper or red pepper for heat. It is cream to light brown, thin enough to drink through a straw, and has a gentle sweet-sour taste.

Traditionally the sweetness comes partly from the grain itself. A little sprouted grain or sweet potato is added to the mash, and its natural enzymes turn some of the starch into sugars. Many sellers also add sugar.

**Kunu gyada (groundnut kunu)**

A thicker, warm or cool drink made from rice cooked in a groundnut (peanut) milk, often with a little tamarind or lemon for tang. It is closer to a light pap than a juice, and very filling. It is not suitable for anyone with a peanut allergy.

**Kunu aya (tigernut kunu)**

A creamy, milky drink made by soaking and blending tigernuts, often with dates, coconut and ginger. It is not grain-based at all. We cover it in depth in our post on [the benefits of kunu aya](/blog/tigernut-kunu-aya-benefits), and the tuber itself in our [tigernut guide](/blog/tigernut-benefits-uses-nigeria).

**Kunu tsamiya and other local variants**

Some households make a sour kunu with **tsamiya** (tamarind), others use wheat (alkama), maize or acha (fonio). You may also hear **kunun dawa** for a sorghum version. These are less common in Lagos but worth trying if you visit the North.

## Kunu vs pap (ogi or akamu): what is the difference?

Both start from fermented grain, which is why people mix them up.

- **Pap (ogi, akamu)** is a thick, smooth paste made from fermented maize, millet or sorghum. You stir it into hot water and eat it with a spoon, often with akara or moin moin.
- **Kunu zaki** is thinner, spiced and drunk cold. The grain is mashed with hot water, briefly fermented, then strained.

## What happens during fermentation?

Kunu zaki is usually left for a short time (often overnight) before it is strained. During that time, friendly lactic acid bacteria multiply and give it the light sour note. An [older study of kunun-zaki made in northern Nigeria](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24414898/) found that by the end of fermentation, Lactobacillus species dominated the drink.

That sounds like a probiotic drink, and you will see that word used online. Be careful with the claim. Most bottled kunu has not been tested for live cultures, and the amount and type of bacteria varies from batch to batch. Enjoy the taste; do not rely on it as a probiotic.

## Is kunu good for you?

Kunu zaki is basically grain, water and spice. That makes it:

- **Filling.** Grain starch gives slow energy, which is why kunu is a traditional breakfast and fasting drink in the North.
- **Dairy-free.** Kunu zaki and kunu aya contain no milk, so they suit people who avoid lactose.
- **Warming in flavour.** The ginger and cloves give a pleasant heat. Ginger is well studied for nausea, according to the [NIH National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ginger).

Millet and sorghum are whole grains with fibre, B vitamins and minerals. How much survives depends on how much of the grain is strained out, which for kunu zaki is a lot. Fibre is mostly left behind in the chaff. If whole-grain benefits matter to you, eating millet or sorghum as a meal does more than drinking kunu.

The big variable is sugar. Many commercial kunus are heavily sweetened. The [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news/item/04-03-2015-who-calls-on-countries-to-reduce-sugars-intake-among-adults-and-children) advises limiting free sugars, so choose lightly sweetened kunu or make your own.

## Food safety: why kunu can upset your stomach

Kunu is a nourishing drink for bacteria too. Studies of kunu sold in Nigerian markets have often found high levels of microbes, usually traced to unclean water, unwashed equipment and poor storage. Kunu kept in a warm bucket for a full day in Lagos heat is a risk.

Simple rules:

- Buy from sellers who keep kunu in ice or a fridge, not in the sun.
- Avoid kunu that smells strongly sour, tastes fizzy or has separated badly with a bad smell.
- Shake well. Some settling is normal.
- Refrigerate home-made kunu straight away and drink within a day or two.
- Pregnant women, young children, older adults and anyone with a weak immune system should be extra careful with street drinks.

## How to make kunu zaki at home

This makes about two litres. Ask an aunty from the North and she will have her own version.

1. Wash two cups of millet (or half millet, half sorghum) and soak in clean water for 24 hours, changing the water once.
2. Drain and blend with a thumb of fresh ginger, a teaspoon of cloves and a little water into a smooth paste.
3. Split the paste into two parts: about three quarters and one quarter.
4. Pour boiling water over the larger part while stirring, until it thickens like light pap.
5. Let it cool to warm, then stir in the smaller, uncooked part. Its natural enzymes thin the mix and add sweetness.
6. Cover and leave in a cool place for a few hours or overnight.
7. Strain through a clean cloth, adding clean water until it is drinkable.
8. Sweeten lightly if you like, chill and drink within a day or two.

Use clean, safe water throughout and scald your cloth and containers.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is kunu zaki made of?

Kunu zaki is made from millet, sorghum or a mix, with ginger and cloves. Part of the grain is cooked with hot water, mixed with uncooked grain paste, briefly fermented and strained. Many sellers add sugar.

### What is the difference between kunu zaki and kunu aya?

Kunu zaki is a thin, spiced grain drink made from millet or sorghum. Kunu aya is a creamy drink made from tigernuts, often with dates and coconut. They taste very different.

### Is kunu a probiotic drink?

Kunu zaki is lightly fermented, and studies have found lactic acid bacteria in it. But bottled kunu is rarely tested for live cultures, and amounts vary. Enjoy it as a drink rather than relying on it as a probiotic.

### Is kunu good for weight loss?

No drink causes weight loss on its own. Kunu is filling, which can help you snack less, but sweetened kunu carries sugar and calories. Choose lightly sweetened kunu and keep portions moderate.

### How long does kunu last?

Fresh kunu with no preservatives should be kept cold and drunk within a day or two. If it smells strongly sour, tastes fizzy or has a bad smell, throw it away.

### Why does kunu sometimes cause stomach upset?

Usually because of unclean water, dirty equipment or kunu left warm for hours. Buy kunu that has been kept in ice or a fridge, and make it at home with safe water and clean containers.
