No juice "boosts" your immune system on its own. Your immune cells need nutrients such as vitamin C, and juices made from orange, guava, pineapple and lemon are easy sources. A large review found vitamin C may slightly shorten colds but does not stop most people catching them. Sleep, a varied diet and vaccines matter more.

When the rainy season starts, or someone in the office starts sneezing, Lagos reaches for pineapple, ginger, lemon and "something with vitamin C". It is a sensible instinct, but the marketing has gone far beyond the science. Here is what your immune system actually needs, what juice can honestly contribute, and when to stop drinking juice and see a doctor.

## What "immunity" really means

Your immune system is not one switch you can turn up. It is a network of cells, organs and signals that respond to germs. Many things affect how well it works: age, sleep, stress, existing illness, vaccines and diet.

The [Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/nutrition-and-immunity/) puts it plainly: the immune system is complex and influenced by a balance of many factors, not by any one specific food or nutrient. It also notes that eating enough nutrients as part of a varied diet is required for healthy immune cells.

So the honest goal is not to "boost" immunity. It is to avoid running short of the nutrients your immune system needs.

## Nutrients your immune system relies on

Harvard lists vitamin C, vitamin D, zinc, selenium, iron and protein among the nutrients identified as critical for immune cells. Juice can help with one of these much more than the others.

**Vitamin C**

The [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminC-Consumer/) explains that vitamin C helps your immune system work properly and acts as an antioxidant. Your body cannot make it or store much of it, so you need some every day. Citrus fruits, guava, pineapple, pawpaw and peppers are all good sources.

**The rest**

Zinc, iron and protein come mainly from beans, fish, meat, eggs and whole grains. Vitamin D comes mostly from sunlight. Juice brings very little of these. That is why a juice can only ever be one part of an immune-friendly diet.

## Does vitamin C juice stop colds?

This is the big question, and there is a good answer. A [large Cochrane review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23440782/) pooled many trials of vitamin C for colds. It found:

- **Taking vitamin C regularly did not reduce how often most people caught colds.**
- **It may slightly shorten how long a cold lasts** in people who take it regularly.
- **Starting vitamin C after a cold begins** did not show a consistent benefit.
- People under heavy physical stress, such as marathon runners, did see fewer colds in some trials.

Most of these trials used vitamin C supplements, not juice. So a daily glass of orange juice may help you meet your needs, but it will not make you cold-proof.

## Ginger, turmeric and other "immune" ingredients

Many immunity juices add ginger and turmeric. Here is what the evidence says:

- **Ginger.** The [NIH's NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ginger) says research shows ginger may help with nausea and vomiting in pregnancy. Most studies tested supplements, not food. There is no good evidence that ginger prevents infections. It does make a warming drink that feels soothing when you are unwell.
- **Turmeric.** [NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/turmeric) says turmeric and its main compound, curcumin, have been studied for many conditions, but the evidence is hard to compare and stronger studies are needed. There is no proof it strengthens immunity. It is a lovely flavour, not a shield.
- **Lemon.** A source of vitamin C, and a bright, sharp taste.

## What juice can honestly do when you feel unwell

When you have a cold, a glass of fresh juice can:

- **Help you stay hydrated**, which matters when you have a fever or a runny nose.
- **Give you some vitamin C** at a time when you may not feel like eating.
- **Soothe a scratchy throat**, especially a ginger-based juice.
- **Feel comforting.** That counts for something.

It cannot cure a cold, flu, malaria, typhoid or COVID-19. It will not replace medicine your doctor prescribes.

## When to see a doctor

In Lagos, a fever is not something to treat with juice alone. See a doctor or go to a clinic if you have:

- A high fever, or any fever with chills, especially if malaria is possible
- Difficulty breathing or chest pain
- A cough lasting more than three weeks
- Severe headache, stiff neck or confusion
- Signs of dehydration, such as very little urine or dizziness
- Any illness in a baby, an older person or someone with a long-term condition that seems to be getting worse

## Habits that matter more than any juice

The [Harvard Nutrition Source](https://nutritionsource.hsph.harvard.edu/nutrition-and-immunity/) highlights a varied diet, enough sleep and managing stress as part of supporting immune health. In practice:

1. **Eat a varied diet**: vegetables, fruit, beans, fish, eggs and whole grains.
2. **Sleep seven or more hours** most nights.
3. **Wash your hands**, especially before eating.
4. **Keep up with vaccines** your doctor recommends.
5. **Move your body** regularly.
6. **Keep juice to a small glass** and drink water through the day.

## How much juice to drink

A small glass a day is plenty. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/5-a-day/5-a-day-what-counts/) suggests a combined total of 150ml of fruit juice, vegetable juice or smoothie a day, with a meal, because juicing releases the fruit's sugars. The [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news/item/04-03-2015-who-calls-on-countries-to-reduce-sugars-intake-among-adults-and-children) counts these as "free sugars" to keep low. Drinking more juice when you are ill does not mean more protection.

If you have diabetes, take blood thinners (ginger and turmeric may matter), are pregnant, or take regular medicine, talk to your doctor before making any juice a daily habit.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which juice is best for immunity?

A juice made with vitamin C fruits such as orange, guava, pineapple or lemon helps you meet your daily vitamin C needs, which your immune cells use. No juice "boosts" immunity on its own. A varied diet, sleep and vaccines matter more.

### Does orange juice help fight a cold?

It can help you stay hydrated and give you vitamin C. A large Cochrane review found regular vitamin C may slightly shorten colds but does not stop most people catching them, and starting it after a cold begins shows no consistent benefit.

### Is ginger and turmeric juice good for the immune system?

Ginger may help with nausea, and turmeric is being studied for several conditions, but there is no good evidence that either strengthens your immune system. They make a warming, tasty juice. Check with your doctor if you take blood thinners.

### How much vitamin C juice should I drink per day?

A small glass, around 150ml, with a meal is enough for most adults. Drinking more does not give more protection and adds sugar. Whole fruit is another good way to get vitamin C, with fibre.

### Can juice cure malaria or typhoid?

No. Malaria and typhoid need proper testing and treatment from a doctor. Juice can help you stay hydrated while you recover, but it is not a treatment. If you have a fever in Lagos, get tested.
