Pineapple and ginger juice gives your body vitamin C, fluid and two well-studied plant compounds: bromelain from pineapple and gingerol from ginger. Research suggests ginger may ease nausea and support digestion, and vitamin C supports normal immune function. It will not cure a cold, but it is a refreshing, nourishing glass.

Ask any Nigerian aunty what to drink when a cold is coming and you will hear the same answer: **pineapple and ginger**. Below is what that glass actually does in your body, what the science says (and does not say), how much to drink, and who should be careful.

## What is in pineapple and ginger juice?

Three things do most of the work.

- **Vitamin C.** Pineapple is a good source. Your body cannot make vitamin C, so it has to come from food. It helps protect cells, helps you absorb iron and supports normal immune function, according to the [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminC-Consumer/).
- **Bromelain.** A group of enzymes found in pineapple that break down protein. The [NIH National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH)](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/bromelain) notes it has been studied for swelling and inflammation, with mixed results.
- **Gingerol.** The compound that gives fresh ginger its heat. Ginger is one of the most researched kitchen remedies, especially for nausea, as summarised by [NCCIH's ginger page](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ginger).

You also get water, natural fruit sugars and small amounts of potassium and other plant compounds.

## 7 benefits of pineapple and ginger juice

Here is what pineapple and ginger may do for your body, with the honest strength of the evidence for each.

**1. It may help digestion**

Bromelain breaks down protein, which is why pineapple has long been used to tenderise meat. Ginger has a long traditional use for an unsettled stomach. Many people find a small glass after a heavy meal of rice and stew sits well. The research on digestion is still limited, so think of it as a gentle helper, not a treatment.

**2. Ginger may ease nausea**

This is ginger's best-supported use. [NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/ginger) says research shows ginger may help with nausea and vomiting in pregnancy. Evidence for other kinds of nausea, such as motion sickness, is weaker. If you are pregnant, ask your doctor or midwife before using ginger regularly.

**3. It may support the body's response to inflammation**

Bromelain and gingerol have both been studied for inflammation. NCCIH notes some studies suggest oral bromelain may reduce swelling after wisdom tooth surgery, but for most other uses there is "very little research". A glass of juice is not a painkiller, so keep your expectations modest.

**4. Vitamin C supports normal immune function**

Vitamin C plays a part in how your immune system works, as the [NIH vitamin C fact sheet](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminC-Consumer/) explains. A fresh-pressed glass is an easy way to get some every day, especially if you do not eat much fruit.

**5. It keeps you hydrated in Lagos heat**

Between the sun, the traffic and long days on the road, it is easy to get dehydrated in Lagos. Juice is mostly water, and the [Mayo Clinic](https://www.mayoclinic.org/healthy-lifestyle/nutrition-and-healthy-eating/in-depth/water/art-20044256) notes that drinks like juice count towards your daily fluid. Plain water should still be your main drink, because juice carries sugar.

**6. It may help ease cold and phlegm symptoms**

A warm, spicy-sweet drink feels good on a scratchy throat, and staying hydrated helps when you have a cold. As for vitamin C, a [large Cochrane review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23440782/) found that taking it regularly did not stop most people catching colds, but it may slightly shorten how long a cold lasts. So pineapple and ginger may help you feel more comfortable. It does not cure a cold.

**7. It gives you antioxidants**

Vitamin C and the compounds in ginger act as antioxidants in the lab. Be careful with big claims, though: [NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/antioxidants-in-depth) points out that antioxidants from whole fruit and vegetables are linked with good health, but antioxidant supplements have not shown the same benefits. Getting them from real fruit is the sensible route.

## Drinking pineapple and ginger juice in the morning

Many people like this juice first thing. Here is what changes when you drink it in the morning:

- **You start the day with fluid.** After a night's sleep, a glass of juice plus water helps you rehydrate.
- **The ginger wakes you up.** That warm kick is a nice alternative to a third cup of coffee.
- **It is better with food.** On an empty stomach, the acid in pineapple and the heat of ginger can cause heartburn in some people. Have it with breakfast, such as eggs, akara or oats, rather than alone.

The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/5-a-day/5-a-day-what-counts/) also advises keeping fruit juice to mealtimes, because the sugar in juice can affect your teeth.

There is no strong evidence that morning is "better" than any other time for health. Drink it when it suits you and when you will enjoy it.

## Pineapple juice vs pineapple ginger juice for phlegm

People often ask which is better when they are coughing up phlegm. The honest answer is that neither has strong human evidence for clearing phlegm.

- **Plain pineapple juice** brings vitamin C and bromelain. Bromelain has been studied for sinus problems, but [NCCIH](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/bromelain) says there is not enough high-quality research to recommend it for sinusitis.
- **Pineapple ginger juice** adds ginger's warmth, which many people find soothing on the throat, plus ginger's well-supported help with nausea when you feel unwell.

If you have to choose, pineapple ginger juice gives you a bit more. But the best things for a cold are still rest, fluids and time. See a doctor if a cough lasts more than a few weeks, if you have a high fever or chest pain, or if you are coughing up blood.

## How much to drink, and who should be careful

**How much:** a small glass a day is enough. The NHS suggests keeping fruit juice and smoothies to about 150ml a day in total, because juicing releases the fruit's sugars. The [World Health Organization](https://www.who.int/news/item/04-03-2015-who-calls-on-countries-to-reduce-sugars-intake-among-adults-and-children) counts sugars in fruit juice as "free sugars" that adults and children should limit.

**Who should be careful:**

- **People on blood thinners or other regular medicine.** Some sources list ginger and bromelain as possible interactions with blood-thinning drugs. NCCIH advises talking to your health care provider before using either if you take any medicine.
- **Pregnant or breastfeeding women.** Ginger may help pregnancy nausea, but check with your doctor or midwife first. Little is known about bromelain in pregnancy.
- **People with acid reflux or heartburn.** Pineapple is acidic and ginger can cause heartburn in some people. If you have [reflux](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/acid-reflux-ger-gerd-adults), try a small amount with food first.
- **People with diabetes or watching their sugar.** Even with no added sugar, juice raises blood sugar faster than whole fruit. Keep portions small and have it with a meal.
- **Anyone with a pineapple allergy.** Skip it.

If you have a medical condition or take regular medication, talk to your doctor before making any juice a daily habit.

## Fresh-pressed vs from concentrate

Most "pineapple ginger" drinks on supermarket shelves are made from concentrate. The juice is heated, the water removed, then water, sugar and flavouring are added back. Heat damages vitamin C and bromelain, and added sugar is the last thing you need in a daily drink.

Cold-pressed juice is made by pressing fresh fruit without heat, then bottling and chilling it straight away. We cold-press fresh pineapple with raw ginger root, so the flavour and the kick survive into the bottle. Read more in [why cold-pressed juice is different](/blog/why-cold-pressed).

The trade-off is shelf life. Fresh juice has no preservatives, so it must stay cold and be drunk within days, not months.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does pineapple and ginger do to the body?

It gives your body fluid, vitamin C, and two plant compounds: bromelain from pineapple and gingerol from ginger. Research suggests ginger may ease nausea, and vitamin C supports normal immune function. Evidence for other benefits, such as digestion and inflammation, is still limited.

### What are the benefits of pineapple and ginger juice?

The main benefits are hydration, vitamin C, possible help with nausea from the ginger, and a gentle aid to digestion. It is also a tasty way to get more fruit into your day. It does not cure or prevent any disease.

### What are the benefits of pineapple and ginger juice in the morning?

In the morning it helps you rehydrate after sleep and the ginger gives you a warm wake-up. Drink it with breakfast rather than on an empty stomach, as the acid and spice can cause heartburn in some people. There is no strong evidence that morning is better than other times.

### Is pineapple juice or pineapple ginger juice better for phlegm?

Neither has strong evidence for clearing phlegm. Pineapple ginger juice adds ginger's soothing warmth, so many people prefer it when they have a cold. Rest and fluids matter most, and see a doctor if a cough lasts more than a few weeks.

### Is pineapple and ginger juice good for a cold?

It may help you feel more comfortable by keeping you hydrated and soothing your throat. A large review found regular vitamin C may slightly shorten colds but does not stop most people catching them. It is not a cure.

### What is ginger and pineapple good for?

It is good for a refreshing, hydrating drink that adds vitamin C to your diet. Ginger is best known for easing nausea. Keep it to a small glass a day, and talk to your doctor first if you are pregnant, take blood thinners or have reflux.
